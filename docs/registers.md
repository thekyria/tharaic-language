# The Two Registers

[← Overview](../README.md)

Tharaic is spoken in two registers. **Everyday** Tharaic is the language described in most of these docs: the speech of the harbor, the market, the home and the oral tale. The **high register** is the language of poetry, learning and worship. It is still Tharaic, but it takes its prestige vocabulary, some of its sounds and a few grammatical habits from **literary Doric**.

The two registers relate much as native English words relate to French and Latin ones: *room* and *chamber*, *begin* and *commence*, *wise* and *sapient*. Speakers know both, and choosing one says something about the speaker and the occasion.

---

## Where each register is used

| Everyday | High |
|----------|------|
| home, harbor, market, ship's deck | schools, poetry and song, philosophy, mathematics and medicine |
| oral tales, rowing chants, proverbs | written literature, speeches, law and public inscriptions |
| prayers said at home | worship, theology and scripture |
| talking to equals and children | formal letters, polite address, talking to strangers of rank |

The old sacred register is now a **style of the high register**: temple speech uses high-register words and grammar, and it still uses chant forms when it is sung ([§ Worship](#worship)). The [Sacred Register](sacred.md) page describes the older temple style and will be revised to match.

---

## Two Greek streams

Tharaic borrowed from Greek-analog speech twice, from two dialects and by two routes.

| | Sailors' Greek | Literary Doric |
|--|----------------|----------------|
| **Dialect** | an Ionic-analog dialect, with *η* | a Doric-analog dialect, with *ᾱ* where the other has *η* |
| **Route** | spoken contact in the ports | books, schools and poets |
| **When** | from Old Tharaic on | Classical Tharaic, after the sound changes in [History](history.md#2-sound-changes) |
| **Adaptation** | fitted to native endings and syllables: *σεληνᾱ*, *ͱημερον*, *δεμᾱ* | keeps its Doric shape, clusters and some endings: *σελᾱνᾱ*, *ͱᾱμερᾱ*, *δᾱμος*, *ψυχᾱ* |
| **Register** | everyday | high |
| **In the lexicon** | origin *Greek* | origin *Doric* |
| **English analog** | Old Norse loans (*sky*, *take*) | French and Latin loans (*chamber*, *sapient*) |

The prestige of Doric comes from learning, not conquest. In the Mediterranean-analog world, Doric is the dialect of choral poetry, of the great medical schools, and of a philosophical school of number and harmony, much as real Doric was the language of Pindar's odes, of the doctors of Cos and of the Pythagoreans. Tharaic families sent their sons to Doric schools, and Tharaic poets learned their meters from Doric songs. When temple scholars began to write theology, they borrowed its words too.

Because the learned words came after changes 2–4, none of those changes apply to them.

## Doublets

The lexicon has about 290 high-register words. About 180 of them have an everyday counterpart, and the lexicon links each pair (↔ in the [Lexicon](lexicon.md)).

| Kind | Everyday | High | English parallel |
|------|----------|------|------------------|
| Native word ↔ Doric word | *χαδαρον* "inner room" | *θαλαμος* "chamber" | room / chamber |
| | *βερειν* "create" | *κτιζειν* "found, create" | make / create |
| | *χακαμον* "wisdom", *χακαμικος* "wise" | *σοφιᾱ*, *σοφος* | wisdom / sapience |
| | *νεφεϸᾱ* "breath, living soul" | *ψυχᾱ* "soul" | breath / spirit |
| Sailors' Greek ↔ Doric, from the same Greek word | *ͱημερον* "day" | *ͱᾱμερᾱ* | *chief* / *chef*: one French word, borrowed twice |
| | *σεληνᾱ* "moon" | *σελᾱνᾱ* | |
| | *δεμᾱ* "the people" | *δᾱμος* "the citizen body" | |
| Doric ↔ its own folk form | *ισχολᾱ* "schoolhouse" | *σχολᾱ* "school, learned leisure" | *sir* / *sire* |

- **Everyday words are not wrong in high speech.** Most of them belong to both registers: *νεφεϸᾱ* is still the word for "breath" and the base of the reflexive. Only a few are marked *everyday* in the lexicon, because they sound low in high speech. These are mainly the sailors'-Greek words with *η* that have a Doric twin with *ᾱ*.
- **The high word is often narrower.** *θαλαμος* is a bedchamber or the inner room of a temple, never a storeroom. *δᾱμος* is the people as a body of citizens, the people of law and oratory.
- **A whole field can be high.** The everyday language never had words for some things, so it uses the high words for them, the way English has no native word for *music* or *theory*: *φιλοσοφιᾱ*, *ρυθμος*, *κιθαρᾱ*, *ιατρικᾱ*.
- **Folk forms.** When a high-register word comes into everyday use, it is adapted like the [later loans](history.md#later-loans) were: a prothetic *ι-* before σ + consonant, or a copy vowel inside other clusters. *σχολᾱ* became *ισχολᾱ*, the village schoolhouse.

### Where the high words are

| Field | Examples |
|-------|----------|
| Poetry, music and letters | *ποιητᾱς* poet (↔ *ϸερος* singer), *αειδειν* sing, *μωσα* Muse, *ͱαρμονιᾱ* harmony, *γραμμα* letter, *βιβλιον* book (↔ *σαφαρον*), *λογος* word, reason (↔ *λακον*) |
| Philosophy, mathematics and schooling | *φιλοσοφος* (↔ *χᾱκιμος* sage), *αριθμος* number, *μαθητᾱς* pupil (↔ *λεμεδος*), *διδασκαλος* teacher (↔ *λᾱμιδος*), *φυσις* nature, *αλᾱθεια* truth (↔ *αληθον*) |
| Virtues and feelings | *αρετᾱ* virtue, *δικαιοσυνᾱ* justice (↔ *σαδακον*), *ͱᾱδονᾱ* pleasure, *οργᾱ* wrath (↔ *αφον*), *ͱυβρις* insolence |
| Worship and theology | *ͱιαρος* sacred (↔ *καδοϸος*), *πνευμα* spirit (↔ *ρῡχᾱ*), *πιστις* faith (↔ *αμανον*), *ͱαμαρτιᾱ* sin (↔ *χατον*), *προφᾱτᾱς* prophet (↔ *νᾱβιος*), *ευανγελιον* gospel (↔ *βασαρον*) |
| City, law and war | *πολις* city-state (↔ *καρτον* town), *πολιτᾱς* citizen, *δικᾱ* lawsuit, *κριτᾱς* judge (↔ *ϸᾱφιτος*), *ͱᾱλιᾱ* assembly, *στρατᾱγος* general |
| Medicine and the body | *ιατρος* physician (↔ *ρεφος* healer), *νοσος* disease, *φαρμακον* remedy, *κεφαλᾱ* head (↔ *ραϸον*), *σωμα* body |
| Polite and formal speech | *αρχεσθαι* commence (↔ *ρεϸειν* begin), *αποκρινεσθαι* reply (↔ *ϙενειν* answer), *αιτειν* request (↔ *ϸελειν* ask), *χαιρε!* greetings, *γυνᾱ* lady (↔ *εινᾱ* woman) |
| Poetic nature words | *θαλασσα* the sea (↔ *θᾱρος*), *ανεμος* wind (↔ *ϝελος*), *ͱᾱλιος* sun (↔ *ͱηλιᾱ*), *ωρανος* heaven (↔ *ϸαμον*), *νᾱσος* island (↔ *νησον*) |

## Learned adjectives

English often pairs a native noun with a learned adjective: *moon* and *lunar*, *mouth* and *oral*, *king* and *royal*. Tharaic does the same. The everyday adjective is built with *-ικος* on the noun itself (*θᾱρικος* "maritime"), when there is one at all. The high register uses an adjective borrowed whole from Doric:

| Everyday noun | High adjective | |
|---------------|----------------|--|
| *σεληνᾱ* moon | *σελᾱναιος* | lunar |
| *ͱηλιᾱ* sun | *ͱᾱλιακος* | solar |
| *ϸαμον* sky | *ωρανιος* | heavenly |
| *μᾱλικος* king | *βασιλικος* | royal |
| *καρτον* town | *πολιτικος* civic; *αστειος* urbane | |
| *σαδον* field | *αγροικος* | rustic, boorish |
| *παμον* mouth | *στοματικος* | oral |
| *βασρον* flesh | *σωματικος* bodily; *σαρκικος* carnal | |
| *ηλος* god | *θειος* | divine |
| *χοραφον* winter | *χειμερινος* | wintry, hibernal |
| *ζαͱαρον* gold | *χρυσεος* | golden |
| *βαρζαλον* iron | *σιδᾱρεος* | of iron |

The lexicon has about 55 of them. Each records the everyday noun it goes with (`base`, shown as "adj. for" in the [Lexicon](lexicon.md)). Like English *rustic* and *urbane*, *αγροικος* and *αστειος* show how the learned class saw town and country.

## Pronunciation

The high register sounds different, and speakers hear it at once.

**1. ᾱ for η.** Doric has *ᾱ* where sailors' Greek has *η*. This is the shibboleth of educated speech: *ͱᾱμερᾱ*, *σελᾱνᾱ*, *δᾱμος*.

Speakers who want to sound learned sometimes put *ᾱ* where Doric itself has *η*, saying *ανᾱρ* for *ανηρ* "man". This *hyperdorism* is laughed at, as Greek comedy laughed at false Doric.

**2. Clusters.** Doric words keep the clusters of their source, which everyday syllables do not allow ([Phonotactics](phonology.md#phonotactics--the-no-cluster-rule)):

| Cluster | Examples |
|---------|----------|
| **σ + stop** or **σμ** at the start of a syllable, also with ρ/λ after it (στρ-) | *σχολᾱ* |
| **stop + stop**: πτ, κτ, φθ, χθ | *κτιζειν* |
| **stop + nasal**, and μν | |
| **ψ** /ps/ and **ξ** /ks/ at the start of a word | *ψυχᾱ* |
| any single consonant closing a syllable inside the word | |

To an everyday listener these clusters sound bookish. A careless or mocking speaker breaks them up with a vowel, as the folk forms do.

**3. In chant.** Chant keeps its strict open syllables in both registers. A Doric cluster is broken by a copy of the following vowel, the same rule as for older loans ([History § The chant register](history.md#3-the-chant-register)): *ψυχᾱ* → *πυσυχᾱ*, *κτιζειν* → *κιτιζειν*, *σχολᾱ* → *σοχολᾱ*.

The lexicon gives these chant forms, and `tools/build_lexicon.py` checks high-register words against the high rules. `tools/check_phonotactics.py --high` checks a text against them. Without `--high`, its default scan lists high-register shapes separately from real violations.

## Grammar

The high register keeps Tharaic grammar: the eight cases, VSO order, the particles. It changes a few preferences. The full forms will come with the [grammar step of the roadmap](roadmap.md#plan).

| Feature | Everyday | High |
|---------|----------|------|
| Telling the past | narrative *ϝα-* + present: *ϝα-νᾱϝει* | aorist: *ενᾱϝε*. *ϝα-* becomes a mark of oral tales and folk song |
| Possession | genitive or construct | genitive. The construct survives only in fixed titles and divine names |
| Long sentences | clauses joined by "and", relatives with *ϸε-* | participle phrases and subordinate clauses, in the manner of Greek prose |
| Doric nouns | regular Tharaic endings, in their folk forms | some Doric endings kept, especially the genitive: *ψυχᾱς* "of the soul" (Doric *ψυχᾶς*), not *ψυχις*; *δᾱμω* "of the people" (Doric *δάμω*), not *δᾱμι*. The sea cases keep their Tharaic endings |

### Doric noun classes

Most Doric nouns end like Tharaic ones, in *-ος*, *-ᾱ* (or short *-α*), *-ον* or *-ευς*. Three endings have no Tharaic class, and the lexicon gives them classes of their own:

| Class | Example | Genitive | Kind of word |
|-------|---------|----------|--------------|
| **-ᾱς** (AS) | *ποιητᾱς* poet | *ποιητᾱ* | men and their roles: *μαθητᾱς*, *πολιτᾱς*, *προφᾱτᾱς* |
| **-μα** (MA) | *ποιημα* poem | *ποιηματος* | results and things: *γραμμα*, *σωμα*, *πνευμα* |
| **-ις** (IS) | *ποιησις* poetry | *ποιησιος* | actions and qualities: *γνωσις*, *πιστις*, *πολις* (*χαρις* has *χαριτος*) |

Their full paradigms come with the grammar step of the [roadmap](roadmap.md#plan).

## Worship

Doric learning reached the temple through its scholars. In the high register:

- **Theology uses Doric words**: *ψυχᾱ* for the soul, *κτιζειν* for the creation of the world, *σοφιᾱ* for divine wisdom.
- **Phoenician words survive in fixed formulas and divine names**: *Βαλιλ ϳαμος* "Lord of the Deep" is still his name, but the moon in a hymn is *σελᾱνᾱ*. *ϳαραχᾱ* lives on only in old formulas.
- **Sung liturgy uses chant forms**, so a hymn says *πυσυχᾱ* where a sermon says *ψυχᾱ*.
- **Scripture is translated in the high register**, including the Gospels ([Translating the Gospels](gospels.md), [Mark 1](mark-1.md); both will be revised).

## In the lexicon

`data/lexicon.yaml` marks the registers with these fields:

- `layer: "doric"` for the learned loans. Their gender follows their meaning, not their ending: *θαλαμος* "chamber" is inanimate although it ends in *-ος*. They may use the stem classes AS, MA and IS.
- `register`: `high` for words used only in high speech, `everyday` for words avoided in it. Words without it belong to both.
- `doublet`: the counterpart in the other register. The two entries point to each other.
- `base`: for a learned adjective, the everyday noun it goes with.
